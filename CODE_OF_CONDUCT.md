# Code of conduct

wngmn is one person's project. This document is short on purpose: everything behind it is one
maintainer reading a report and deciding what to do, and a longer document would describe a
moderation team, an investigation process and an appeals ladder that do not exist here.

## What is expected

Anyone taking part — opening an issue, reviewing a pull request, arguing about a default —
is expected to:

* Engage kindly and honestly, and assume the same of others until shown otherwise.
* Respect different viewpoints and experience, including the reading of the code you disagree
  with.
* Give and take criticism of the work without turning it into criticism of the person.
* Take responsibility when you get something wrong, and repair it where you can.
* Accept that words land differently across cultures, backgrounds and first languages, and
  that "I did not mean it that way" is the start of the repair rather than the end of it.

Not acceptable, anywhere in this project:

* **Harassment** — continuing unwanted personal attention after a clear request to stop.
* **Insults and demeaning comments** aimed at a person or a group rather than at an argument.
* **Discrimination or stereotyping** on the basis of who somebody is: race, ethnicity, caste,
  colour, age, physical characteristics, neurodiversity, disability, sex or gender, gender
  identity or expression, sexual orientation, language, religion or philosophy, national or
  social origin, socio-economic position, or level of education.
* **Sexualised language or attention**, which has nothing to do with anything here.
* **Publishing private information** without permission. This one is not abstract for this
  project: wngmn handles interview audio, and a bug report, a test fixture or a screenshot
  can carry what a journalist or a candidate actually said. Redact question text before you
  paste it — the issue template asks for this, and the timings and flags around the text are
  what diagnose a bug anyway.
* **Threats of violence, or encouraging harm** to anyone.
* **Impersonating someone**, or using a second account to get around a block.

## Scope

This applies to the repository — issues, pull requests, commits, discussions, code review —
and to anywhere somebody is plainly representing the project.

## Reporting

There are two routes, and both are honest about what they are:

* **GitHub's private vulnerability reporting on this repository**: Security → Report a
  vulnerability. It opens an advisory that only the maintainer can see. It exists for
  security, but it is the only private channel this project has, so it accepts conduct
  reports too. Say in the first line that it is a conduct report rather than a vulnerability,
  so it is not triaged as one.
* **A direct message on GitHub to the repository owner**, the maintainer named in
  [LICENSE](LICENSE).

There is no project email address, no PGP key, no moderation team and no chat server. Do not
invent one from an address you find elsewhere.

Include what happened, where, and links or permalinks if the thing is still visible. You do
not have to have a view on what should be done about it.

If the report is about the maintainer, there is no neutral party inside this project to send
it to. GitHub's own abuse reporting is the route that does not run through me, and it is the
right one to use.

This is one person's project. Expect an acknowledgement in days rather than hours.

## What happens then

The maintainer reads the report, looks at what is publicly visible, and asks the people
involved for their account if that is needed. There is no investigation beyond that — no
interviewing of witnesses, no reviewing of logs or recordings, because there are no logs or
recordings to review.

What one maintainer can actually do, roughly in order: say privately that something has to
stop; edit, hide, lock or delete a comment, issue or pull request; close a contribution
without merging it; block an account from the repository; and report an account to GitHub.
Most things warrant the first of those. Harassment, a threat, or publishing somebody's
private information — including transcript text from a real interview — warrants a block
without a warning first.

Reports are kept to the people who need to know. A public note may follow if the incident was
public, but not against the wishes of whoever reported it.
